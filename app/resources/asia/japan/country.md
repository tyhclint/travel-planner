# Japan

## Overview
Japan rewards travelers who prepare for small practical details before arrival. Country-wide advice matters because the same patterns show up across Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and smaller towns: public transport is orderly and quiet, cash is still useful, rubbish bins are limited, and many places expect shoes-off etiquette. For first-time visitors, it helps to think of Japan less as "hard to navigate" and more as "easy once you know the rules."

## Before you fly
- Check medication rules before departure. Japan is strict about some prescription and over-the-counter drugs, and some items that are routine elsewhere may require advance paperwork or may not be allowed.
- Pack easy-on, easy-off shoes and good socks. You may be asked to remove shoes in temples, traditional accommodations, some restaurants, and some indoor attractions.
- Bring a physical credit card, not just a mobile wallet. Tap-to-pay works in some large-city chains, but smaller shops and rural areas may still require an actual card or cash.
- Plan for connectivity early: eSIM, pocket Wi-Fi, or a roaming package. Reliable mobile data makes navigation, translation, and train changes much easier.
- Carry a power bank and the correct travel adapter. Heavy navigation and translation use can drain your phone quickly.
- If you are traveling with children, pack a few days of essentials up front rather than assuming every item will be easy to find immediately after landing.

## Money and payments
- Japan is more card-friendly than it used to be, but cash still matters, especially at smaller restaurants, older businesses, shrines, local transport situations, and some ticket machines.
- Convenience stores and station ATMs are often the easiest places for international cash withdrawal.
- Tipping is not part of normal service culture. Good service is expected, and leaving extra money can create confusion rather than appreciation.

## Transport etiquette
- Keep your phone on silent on trains and buses, and avoid taking calls while onboard.
- Speak quietly in public transport. Trains are treated as shared calm spaces rather than loud social areas.
- Follow local escalator flow. In Tokyo and much of eastern Japan, people usually stand on the left; in Osaka and much of western Japan, they usually stand on the right.
- Learn to recognize the shinkansen symbol in mapping apps and station signage so you do not confuse local rail with long-distance bullet train services.
- Give yourself extra time for station navigation. Major stations are efficient but large, and transfers can involve more walking than first-time visitors expect.

## Food and dining norms
- Chopstick manners matter. Do not stick chopsticks upright into rice and do not pass food from one set of chopsticks to another.
- It is normal to lift rice or soup bowls closer to your face while eating.
- Slurping noodles is acceptable and can even be seen as enjoying the meal properly.
- Calling out `sumimasen` is a normal, polite way to get a server's attention in casual restaurants.
- Saying `itadakimasu` before eating and `gochisousama` after eating is appreciated, though not mandatory for visitors.
- Finish what you order when you can. Leaving a lot of food behind is less ideal than ordering conservatively and adding more if needed.

## Street behavior and cleanliness
- Do not assume you will find public rubbish bins everywhere. It is common to carry your trash until you find a convenience store bin, station bin, vending-machine bin, or your accommodation.
- Eating while walking is less common than in many other countries. A better default is to eat near where you bought the food or while seated in an appropriate place.
- Long-distance trains are an exception: eating on the shinkansen is normal, and station bento are made for that use case.

## Onsen and bathing
- Traditional onsen etiquette usually requires washing thoroughly before entering the bath.
- Baths are typically enjoyed nude, and swimwear is generally not used.
- Tattoo rules vary. Some properties still restrict tattooed guests, while others are more flexible, so check in advance rather than assuming access.

## Planning rhythm
- Some attractions, cafes, and smaller businesses open later than visitors expect, so not every sightseeing day needs a very early start.
- Double-check opening days for cultural sites and independent businesses, especially on Mondays, Tuesdays, or the day after a public holiday.
- If a specific experience matters a lot to your trip, reserve it early. This is especially true for seasonal attractions, popular museums, themed experiences, and some rail-adjacent travel logistics.

## Souvenirs and gifts
- Good souvenirs in Japan tend to be compact, practical, and recognizably local rather than oversized novelty items.
- Easy gift categories include Japanese sweets, chopsticks, small ceramics, character goods, stationery, socks, and regional crafts.
- Limited-edition convenience store items and branded seasonal snacks can work surprisingly well as casual gifts.
- Personalized or locally themed items often feel more thoughtful than generic airport souvenirs.
- If you plan to buy electronics, treat that as a separate research task rather than an impulse souvenir purchase.

## Helpful mindset
- You are not expected to know every unwritten rule. A calm pace, quiet public behavior, and visible effort to be considerate go a long way.
- When in doubt, watch what local people are doing and copy the flow.
- Translation apps, Google Maps, and a little patience solve most day-to-day travel friction.
