# Tokyo, Japan

## Overview
Tokyo is a massive metropolitan city best explored by grouping sights geographically rather than trying to see everything in one pass. A minimum of 5 days is recommended for a first visit, since the city is significantly larger and more spread out than most first-time visitors expect.

## Best time to visit
- **Spring (late March–April)**: cherry blossom (sakura) season, especially notable at Ueno Park and Shinjuku Gyoen.
- **Autumn (November)**: momiji (autumn foliage) season, also strong at Shinjuku Gyoen.
- **Weekends**: some experiences are weekend-specific — Harajuku's street fashion crowd is more visible on weekends, and Akihabara closes its main street to cars from 1–6 PM on Sundays for pedestrian-only browsing.
- **Rush hour to avoid**: weekday trains are extremely crowded 8–10 AM and 6–8 PM.

## Getting there (airports)
Two main airports serve Tokyo:
- **Haneda (HND)**: located within Tokyo itself, roughly 30 minutes to Shibuya by train. Generally the more convenient option.
- **Narita (NRT)**: located in Chiba, 40–90 minutes to central Tokyo, typically requires a separate airport express ticket (Narita Express or Keisei Skyliner). Sometimes worth it for cheaper flights.
- Private transfers and airport limousine buses are available from both airports and are recommended for late-night arrivals (local trains stop around midnight) or groups with heavy luggage.

## Connectivity
- A local eSIM or prepaid SIM is strongly recommended for continuous data access.
- Pocket Wi-Fi rental is a good option for groups with multiple devices.
- Free public Wi-Fi covers many central/popular areas but not comprehensively.

## Getting around
- **IC cards** (Suica or Pasmo — functionally identical) are the standard way to pay for trains, buses, and even convenience store purchases. iPhone users can add a virtual IC card via the Wallet app; Android support is limited to phones purchased in Japan.
- **Google Maps** works well for Tokyo public transit routing, including fare estimates.
- **Trains stop running around midnight** — plan to be at the station by 11:30 PM at the latest if changing lines. Taxis are expensive as a late-night fallback.
- A **JR Pass is not needed** if staying within Tokyo only; it becomes worthwhile if also traveling to other cities (e.g. Kyoto, Osaka) by shinkansen.
- Basic train etiquette: move fully into the train car rather than blocking doors, keep backpacks in front of you when crowded, and avoid talking loudly or letting headphone audio leak.

## Where to stay
First-time visitors are generally best based near **Shibuya, Shinjuku, or Asakusa** — look for accommodation within a 10-minute walk of a train station.
- **Budget**: hostels/poshtels in Asakusa; business hotels near Shimbashi station.
- **Mid-range**: hotels in Ginza (convenient, central, near flagship shopping) or Shinagawa (close to both Shibuya and Haneda Airport).
- **Luxury**: hotels in Roppongi (elevated city views) or similar high-end districts.

## Suggested geographic groupings (multi-day itinerary logic)
Rather than a strict day-by-day sequence, group sights by proximity and mix-and-match:

**Yoyogi Park / Harajuku / Omotesando / Roppongi**
Best on a weekend. Yoyogi Park and Meiji Jingu shrine (a Shinto shrine with a water purification ritual at the entrance) connect to Harajuku's Takeshita Dori shopping street, known for crepes and trendy youth fashion. Continuing east through Omotesando and Aoyama (upscale shopping, small museums) leads to Roppongi, where an observation deck (Tokyo City View) offers rooftop skyline views, best near sunset.

**Shibuya and surroundings**
Home to the famous Shibuya Scramble Crossing and the Hachiko statue (a popular meeting point). Shibuya Sky is a well-regarded observation deck requiring advance ticket booking. The area is a major shopping district; Dogenzaka nearby is known for nightlife (ramen shops, izakaya bars). Quieter, more residential neighboring areas include Shimo-Kitazawa, Daikanyama, Naka-Meguro, and Ebisu.

**Akihabara / Ueno Park / Asakusa**
Best on a Sunday afternoon when Akihabara's main street closes to cars. Akihabara is known for electronics, arcades, and anime/manga culture. Ueno Park is popular during cherry blossom season. Asakusa is a backpacker-friendly district centered on Senso-ji, a major Buddhist temple, with the adjoining Nakamise shopping street for food and souvenirs. Tokyo Skytree, one of the tallest structures in the world, is about 20 minutes from Asakusa and includes an aquarium and planetarium nearby.

**Shinjuku**
A large area worth a dedicated day. Highlights include Shinjuku Gyoen (a national garden, entry fee applies, especially popular in spring/autumn), the free observation deck at the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building, Omoide Yokocho (a nostalgic alley of small izakaya stalls), and Golden Gai at night (a dense cluster of tiny themed bars, each typically charging a cover fee).

**Toyosu / Ginza / Imperial Palace area**
An early start is recommended to see the tuna auction at Toyosu Market (the market that replaced the original Tsukiji site), viewable from a public gallery from around 5 AM; the market is closed Sundays and public holidays. Fresh sushi breakfast stalls are available on-site, and the original Tsukiji Outer Market nearby still operates for food. Nearby digital art museums (teamLab Borderless at Azabudai Hills, teamLab Planets at Toyosu) require advance timed-entry tickets. Ginza offers high-end shopping and flagship stores for Japanese brands like Uniqlo and MUJI. The Imperial Palace offers guided tours by advance booking; nearby Atago Shrine and Hie Shrine are notable smaller cultural sites.

## Day trips from Tokyo (under ~3 hours)
Popular options include Hakone, Nikko, Kamakura, and the Fuji Five Lakes area — suitable as day trips or short overnight extensions for travelers wanting a break from the city or mountain/nature scenery (including Mount Fuji views where weather permits).

## Food notes
- Convenience stores ("konbini" — 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Lawson) are a genuinely useful and affordable food source, especially for onigiri (rice balls).
- Ramen, tonkatsu (breaded pork cutlet), tsukemen (dipping ramen), and izakaya-style skewers are widely available across most districts covered above.
- Popular sit-down spots tend to have long queues at peak meal times; off-peak visits (early lunch, mid-afternoon) reduce wait times significantly.
- Cash is still required at some smaller/older establishments, particularly for specialty restaurants.

## Practical tips
- Google Translate's camera-based translation is useful for Japanese-only menus.
- Visit Japan Web (Japan's online immigration/customs service) should be completed before arrival.
- Tokyo is generally considered very safe, including for solo travelers, with the notable exception of bar-related scams in Kabukicho at night.
- Comfortable walking shoes are important — Tokyo is very walkable and taxis are costly.
