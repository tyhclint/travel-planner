# Kyoto Five-Day Itinerary

Five days in Kyoto is the perfect amount of time to spend in Kyoto. You can explore the main sightseeing districts and take a daytrip to Nara. This itinerary allows you to get the best out of five full days in the city.

## First: A Few Things to Keep in Mind
The first two days of this itinerary cover Kyoto's most popular sightseeing districts: Southern Higashiyama and Arashiyama. This is a good itinerary to do in the off-season (summer or winter). In high-season (spring and fall), it may be too crowded for comfort.

If you'll be in town during cherry blossom season, check out dedicated cherry blossom itineraries. Spring (late March–April) and autumn (November) are the most scenic times to visit, but also the busiest [citation:3].

## Itinerary Summary
**Duration**: Five days of 6 to 8 hours of touring (not including evening strolls)
**Areas Covered**: Southern Higashiyama, Downtown, Fushimi, Gion, Arashiyama, Central Kyoto, Kurama/Kibune, Northern Higashiyama, and Nara
**Sights visited**: Kiyomizu-dera Temple, Sannen-zaka and Ninen-zaka, Chion-in Temple, Shoren-in Temple, Nishiki Market, Fushimi-Inari-Taisha Shrine, Gion Shimbashi, Tenryu-ji Temple, Arashiyama Bamboo Grove, Okochi-Sanso Villa, Kinkaku-ji Temple (Golden Pavilion), Daitoku-ji Temple, Kurama-dera Temple, Kibune, Ginkaku-ji Temple, the Path of Philosophy, Nanzen-ji Temple, Todai-ji Temple (Nara), Nara Park, Kasuga Taisha Shrine (Nara)

## Day 1: Southern Higashiyama, Downtown and Fushimi-Inari

**8:30am: Kiyomizu-dera Temple**
Start your day early at Kiyomizu-dera, one of Kyoto's most iconic temples. Take a bus or taxi to the Gojo-Kawaramachi intersection (closest bus stop: Gojozaka) and walk up Gojo-zaka hill. For a more pleasant approach, bear right up Chawan-zaka to avoid traffic. Enter the temple and fully explore it—don't forget to visit the Tainai-Meguri (a dark passage inside the temple said to represent the womb of a bodhisattva). Arriving early is essential to beat the crowds [citation:7].

**9:30am: Sannen-zaka and Ninnen-zaka, then Maruyama-koen Park**
After exploring Kiyomizu-dera, exit via the front and walk down Matsubara-dori Street to reach the lovely preserved district of Sannen-zaka Hill. Continue down to Ninnen-zaka, another beautifully preserved street with traditional wooden storefronts [citation:11]. These stone-paved lanes are at their best before mid-morning when crowds are thinner. Turn right into Nene-no-Michi, which is mostly free from cars, and follow it north to reach Maruyama-koen Park.

**10:30am: Chion-in Temple**
Exit the north side of Maruyama-koen and follow the street north to the impressive main gate of Chion-in Temple, the head temple of the Jodo sect of Buddhism. Climb the steep steps and enter the wide courtyard. The main hall is under construction and will be for several years, so you don't have to linger here.

**11:00am: Shoren-in Temple**
You'll soon pass the enormous camphor trees in front of Shoren-in Temple. Enter this quieter temple and enjoy a cup of green tea and a sweet while looking out over the garden.

**11:45am: Travel to Downtown Kyoto**
Exit Shoren-in and walk north (downhill) to reach Sanjo-dori Street. Walk west to Higashiyama Station on the Tozai subway line. Take it two stops west and get off at Kyoto Shiyakushomae in downtown.

**Noon: Lunch Downtown**
Downtown Kyoto offers an almost endless selection of places to eat, from soba and udon to set meals. Look for lunch specials, which are often excellent value compared with dinner [citation:7].

**1:00pm: Nishiki Market**
After lunch, make your way to the east end of Nishiki Market (where it joins the Teramachi Shopping Arcade). This 400-meter-long covered market has over 130 stalls and is known as "Kyoto's Kitchen" [citation:6]. It's usually crowded; if you get tired of the crowds, you can bail by walking one block south to Shijo-dori Street. Try local specialties like tamagoyaki (rolled omelet) or pick up seasonal ingredients.

**2:00pm: Daimaru Basement Food Floor**
After emerging from the west end of Nishiki, continue straight to Daimaru Department Store. Go downstairs to the basement food floor, which offers an incredible array of Japanese sweets, teas, and prepared foods. It's a great place to see the artistry of Japanese cuisine.

**2:30pm: Take the Keihan Line to Fushimi-Inari Station**
Exit Daimaru via the main entrance onto Shijo-dori Street. Walk east across the Kamo-gawa River to Keihan Gion-Shijo Station. Take the Keihan Line south to Fushimi-Inari Station. Note that limited express trains do not stop at Fushimi-Inari, so take a regular or semi-express.

**3:00pm: Fushimi-Inari-Taisha Shrine**
From the station, walk up the hill and cross the JR tracks. After crossing a major street, you'll see the first torii (gate) of the shrine. This iconic shrine is famous for its thousands of vermilion torii gates that wind up Mount Inari [citation:4]. The entry is free and the shrine is open 24 hours [citation:11]. The hike up to Shin-ike Pond takes about 30-40 minutes and offers a good taste of the torii-lined paths without the full 2-hour summit hike. Early morning or late afternoon (around 4 PM) are ideal times to avoid the main tour groups [citation:12].

**Late Afternoon: Return to Hotel for Rest**
Take either the Keihan Line or the JR Line back from Fushimi-Inari to central Kyoto.

**7:00pm: Dinner Downtown**
Downtown Kyoto has the best selection of restaurants in town—choose from ramen, tonkatsu, izakaya-style skewers, or more formal kaiseki.

**After Dinner: Gion Shimbashi**
After dinner, take an evening walk through Gion. Start from Shijo-Ohashi Bridge (the big bridge on Shijo-dori over the Kamo-gawa River). Cross Kawabata-dori and walk north on the east side for about 100 meters to find a tree-lined pedestrian lane—this is the end of Shirakawa-dori. Follow it east to the Shimbashi District (Shirakawa), the most beautiful street in Kyoto with traditional wooden machiya and lantern-lit canals. From there, make your way south to Hanami-koji, another picturesque lane in geisha territory. Remember: do not chase or photograph geiko or maiko without permission [citation:7].

## Day 2: Arashiyama, Kinkaku-ji and Daitoku-ji

**8:30am: Head west to Arashiyama by taxi, train or bus**
Arashiyama is beautiful but can get crowded quickly. The key is to arrive early [citation:7]. Your choice of transport will depend on where you are staying.

**9:00am: Tenryu-ji Temple**
First, explore Tenryu-ji Temple, the most significant temple in the Arashiyama area and a UNESCO World Heritage Site [citation:9]. Be sure to check out both the inside of the main hall and the expansive stroll garden, which uses the technique of shakkei (borrowed scenery) with the surrounding mountains.

**10:00am: Arashiyama Bamboo Grove**
Leave Tenryu-ji by the north gate (accessible through the garden) and turn left into the famed Arashiyama Bamboo Grove. The path is short but magical. To avoid the worst crowds, walk deeper into the grove—the further you go, the more peaceful it becomes [citation:12].

**10:30am: Okochi-Sanso Villa**
At the top of the hill in the Bamboo Grove, look for a small hut and a road leading up into the forest—this is the entrance to Okochi-Sanso Villa, the former home of a wealthy Japanese actor. The garden is spectacular, with hillside views over Kyoto. Hold onto the ticket you receive, as it gets you a cup of green tea and a sweet after the garden tour [citation:6].

**11:30am: Lunch in Arashiyama**
Return to central Arashiyama and eat a simple lunch of noodles or rice in one of the shokudo (casual restaurants) on the main strip near Togetsukyo Bridge [citation:7].

**12:30pm: Taxi to Kinkaku-ji Temple**
Public transport options between Arashiyama and Kinkaku-ji are limited and indirect, so a taxi is recommended. The fare is around ¥2000.

**1:00pm: Kinkaku-ji Temple (Golden Pavilion)**
Kinkaku-ji is one of Kyoto's most famous sights—a Zen temple covered in gold leaf, reflected beautifully in the surrounding pond. It will most likely be crowded, but the walking route through the grounds moves steadily. The main view across the pond is the reason to come [citation:4].

**2:00pm: Taxi to Daitoku-ji Temple**
Again, a taxi is the most efficient option (around ¥2000). If you're feeling templed out by this point, consider skipping Daitoku-ji and heading back to downtown Kyoto by bus from Kinkakuji-michi stop.

**2:30pm: Daitoku-ji Temple**
Daitoku-ji is one of Kyoto's most important Zen temple complexes. It's actually a collection of subtemples, each with its own gardens. Consider visiting Koto-in or Zuiho-in to experience Zen rock gardens and tea houses in a quieter setting.

**3:00pm: Return Downtown**
From Daitoku-ji, it's about a 20-minute walk east on Kitaoji-dori Street to Kitaoji Station on the Karasuma Subway Line, which connects to downtown and Kyoto Station.

**Late Afternoon: Rest at your hotel**

**6:30pm: Dinner Downtown**

**8:00pm: Stroll Downtown**
After dinner, consider an evening stroll along Pontocho Alley, a narrow dining alley between the river and Kiyamachi-dori. It's especially atmospheric at night, with tiny restaurants and lanterns. In warmer months, some restaurants set up riverside terraces [citation:7].

## Day 3: Kurama, Kibune and Northern Higashiyama

**Morning: Kurama and Kibune**
Take a half-day trip to the village of Kurama and hike over the mountain to the village of Kibune. Take the Eizan Railway from Demachiyanagi Station to Kibune-guchi or Kurama. The classic hike from Kurama-dera (a mountaintop temple) over the ridge to Kibune takes about 90 minutes through cedar forest and offers a spiritual experience away from the tourist crowds [citation:6]. In summer, Kibune is famous for kawadoko dining—restaurants with platforms set up over the river [citation:1]. Kurama-dera is considered a major power spot in Japanese spiritual practice—take time to sit on the wooden deck and enjoy the valley view.

**Noon or 1:00pm: Lunch back in Kyoto**
Return by Eizan train to Demachiyanagi and eat lunch in the Hyakumamben area (the intersection of Imadegawa and Higashioji), about 10 minutes' walk east of the station.

**2:00pm: Ginkaku-ji Temple (Silver Pavilion)**
Head east along Imadegawa-dori toward the Higashiyama mountains. Ginkaku-ji, the Silver Pavilion, is known for its sand garden and moss garden. The sand garden—with its meticulously raked patterns—is actually better than the more famous Kinkaku-ji's garden [citation:6]. Unlike its golden counterpart, Ginkaku-ji offers a quiet, contemplative experience.

**3:00pm: The Path of Philosophy**
After exploring Ginkaku-ji, walk south along the Path of Philosophy (Tetsugaku-no-Michi), a 2-kilometer canal path lined with cherry trees. In April it's packed, but in other seasons it's a peaceful stroll [citation:6]. Along the way, consider a detour up the hill to Honen-in, a tiny temple with a thatched gate and gravel courtyard that most visitors miss.

**4:00pm: Nanzen-ji Temple**
Continue south to Nanzen-ji, a massive temple complex with a 100-year-old aqueduct running through it—a rare sight in a temple setting. Even if the inner garden is closed, the outer grounds are open 24 hours. Walk up the stone steps behind the aqueduct to the smaller subtemples; Tenju-an has a rock garden that rivals Ryoan-ji [citation:6]. From here, walk to Keage Station on the Tozai subway line to return downtown.

## Day 4: Daytrip to Nara

Nara is about 30-40 minutes from Kyoto by direct express trains on the Kintetsu Line, making it an easy and rewarding day trip [citation:5]. The Kintetsu Nara Station is more convenient for sightseeing than JR Nara Station [citation:5].

**Morning: Nara Park and Todai-ji Temple**
Start at Nara Park, where wild deer roam freely—they're considered messengers of the gods and are protected as national treasures [citation:5]. You can feed them special "deer crackers" (shika senbei), but be respectful as they can be assertive.

Walk deeper into the park to Todai-ji Temple, a UNESCO World Heritage Site housing the Great Buddha (Daibutsu) in one of the world's largest wooden buildings. The scale is genuinely awe-inspiring [citation:5].

**Kofuku-ji Temple**
On your way back toward the station, stop at Kofuku-ji, a 1300-year-old temple complex with an iconic Five-Story Pagoda [citation:5]. The outer grounds are free to explore; the National Treasure Museum has an entrance fee.

**Lunch**
Have lunch near Nara Park or on Higashimuki Shopping Street, a covered arcade outside Kintetsu Nara Station. Try local specialties like kakinoha-zushi (sushi wrapped in persimmon leaves) [citation:5][citation:10].

**Afternoon: Kasuga Taisha Shrine**
Walk through the forested path lined with over 2,000 stone lanterns to Kasuga Taisha, Nara's most important Shinto shrine. The vermilion gates and lantern-filled approach are some of Nara's most photogenic scenes [citation:10].

**Isuien Garden**
If time permits, visit Isuien Garden, one of the finest strolling gardens in Nara. It uses the technique of shakkei (borrowed scenery), with Mount Wakakusayama forming the natural backdrop [citation:10]. The pond is shaped to read the character for "water" (mizu)—a remarkable landscape design detail.

**Late Afternoon: Naramachi**
Explore Naramachi, the old merchant district with Edo-era wooden townhouses, now converted into cafes, shops, and small museums [citation:5]. Gangoji Temple, one of Japan's oldest temples, is also worth a stop.

**Return to Kyoto**
Trains from Kintetsu Nara Station to Kyoto run until around 10 or 11 PM. Souvenir shops close earlier (around 7-8 PM), so plan accordingly [citation:5].

## Day 5: Your Choice

On your last full day in Kyoto, head a bit off the beaten path. Here are some ideas:

**Nijo Castle**
Explore Nijo Castle, a World Heritage site connected to the Tokugawa shogunate. The Ninomaru Palace is a National Treasure with "nightingale floors" designed to chirp when walked upon [citation:1]. The castle grounds offer a different side of Kyoto's history—less temple-centric and more feudal-era grandeur.

**Ryoan-ji Temple**
Famous for its Zen rock garden with 15 rocks arranged so that only 14 are visible from any single vantage point [citation:8]. This embodies the Zen philosophy of imperfection and the idea that true wealth lies in recognizing that what you have is enough. Sit on the veranda and contemplate—don't just snap a photo and rush off [citation:8].

**Cultural Experiences**
Add a hands-on cultural experience: tea ceremony, kimono rental, or Zen meditation at Myoshin-ji temple [citation:1]. Kyoto is the home of the tea ceremony, and there are many venues offering authentic experiences.

**Shopping**
Shop on Teramachi-dori and Shijo-dori for souvenirs. For stationery lovers, Kyukyodo on Teramachi Street has been purveying fine paper and incense since 1663 [citation:13].

**Lesser-Known Temples**
Visit Tofuku-ji Temple for its stunning hojo garden, or Saiho-ji (the Moss Temple)—but note that Saiho-ji requires advance reservations via mail postcard, a famously cumbersome but worthwhile process [citation:13].

## Practical Tips for Kyoto

**Getting Around**
- An IC card (Suica or Icoca) is the easiest way to pay for trains and buses in Kyoto [citation:11].
- The Subway & Bus One-Day Pass can be useful on days with several subway rides, but an IC card is usually the least stressful choice for first-time visitors [citation:11].
- Comfortable walking shoes are essential—Kyoto is very walkable, with many sights accessible only on foot.

**Etiquette**
- At shrines: pass through the torii gate respectfully, and follow the "two bows, two claps, one bow" form when praying [citation:1].
- At temples: perform gasshō (placing the palms together) and worship quietly.
- Check whether photography is allowed—inside buildings and at Buddhist statues it's often prohibited [citation:1].
- In Gion and residential areas, keep voices down and do not enter private property.
- Don't take photos of geiko or maiko without permission [citation:7].

**When to Visit**
- Spring (late March–April) and autumn (November) are the most beautiful but also the most crowded seasons [citation:3].
- Weekday trains and buses can be crowded during rush hour (8–10 AM and 6–8 PM).
- Many temples open early (around 8 AM)—arrive at opening time to beat the crowds [citation:7].

**Booking in Advance**
- The Sagano Romantic Train in Arashiyama sells out—book one month in advance [citation:11].
- Tea ceremonies, kimono rentals, and small-group cultural workshops should be booked ahead when central to your plans [citation:11].
